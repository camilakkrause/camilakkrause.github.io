Laser Color Model

sRGB

Monochromatic laser light lies outside every standard monitor's RGB gamut, and a bright laser spot triggers visual nonlinearities that a dim color-matching patch does not. This engine models perceived laser appearance in four steps:

  1. Bezold–Brücke High-Luminance Shift (λ → λeff)

    CIE 1931 color matching was measured at moderate brightness. At the high luminance of a real laser spot, cone response compression shifts hues toward three psychophysical invariant wavelengths (474 nm blue, 506 nm blue-green, and 574 nm yellow). In particular, a bright 532 nm Nd:YAG laser shifts warmer into vivid emerald green rather than dim cyan-mint, and a 633 nm HeNe laser picks up its characteristic slight orange-red warmth.

  2. CIE 1931 2° Standard Observer

    The shifted wavelength λeff is evaluated against the tabulated 5 nm CIE 1931 2° color-matching functions x̄(λ), ȳ(λ), z̄(λ) (interpolated to 1 nm) to obtain tristimulus values (X, Y, Z).

  3. Oklab Constant-Hue Projection onto Display P3 / sRGB

    Instead of hard-clipping negative channels or mixing linearly with D65 white (which introduces the Abney effect hue drift), (X, Y, Z) is converted into Oklab to extract perceived hue h(λeff) and projected onto the maximum-chroma ridge of your screen's actual gamut (Display P3 on wide-gamut displays, falling back to sRGB). Deep reds (>615 nm) clamp cleanly at the red primary so 650+ nm lasers never leak blue or turn pinkish.

  4. Photopic Visibility Rolloff

    Because human photopic sensitivity ȳ(λ) falls off steeply in the deep violet (<430 nm) and deep red (>635 nm), linear brightness is rolled off using the physical wavelength's true ȳ(λ) before applying the IEC 61966-2-1 transfer curve—preserving the darkening transition from 633 nm HeNe to 650 nm pointer red to 694 nm ruby.
